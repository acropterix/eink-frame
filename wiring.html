Frame wiring

Adafruit ESP32 Feather V2 → Waveshare 13.3" e-Paper HAT+ (E). Follow the pictures; each step has one diagram.

Pick your board: every diagram and table below redraws for it. Flash the firmware with the same board selected in Arduino IDE.

The whole thing

1Panel → adapter → HAT

✅ No voltage switch on this board. The HAT+ (E) is 3.3 V only — there is nothing to set. (The "3.3V/5V" switch you may have read about is on Waveshare's older driver HAT.)

Hold the panel by its edges. Never crease the orange flex where it meets the glass.

2The 10-wire cable: HAT → board

Plug the cable's small white plug into the HAT. Push each black jumper end onto the board pin shown. Hold both boards exactly like this: HAT text readable, board with USB-C at the top and the metal shieldcomponents and pin labels facing you.

⚠️ Before plugging in USB: follow each wire from its printed label on the HAT to the board pin — go by the label, not the colour. Red must sit under VCC and go to 3V3; black under GND to GND. Then power up and touch both boards after 10 s — anything hot, unplug.
⚡ Keep the VCC and GND jumpers short and direct (≈10 cm or less), board straight to HAT. The panel draws current in bursts; long or shared power leads make it show only yellow and black.
Test now: USB-C into the Mac, press the board's reset button (R on the XIAOreset on the Feather). The panel flickers for ~20 s and shows a picture.

3The push button

One button does everything: short press = next picture, hold 3 s = config mode (hold 3 s again, or press Restart on the page, to leave; it also ends by itself after 30 min). No switch, LED or resistor needed.

  1. One button leg → A0 (left row, 5th pin from the top, just below GND). No resistor: the firmware turns on the pull-up.
  2. Other leg → GND (left row, 4th pin). Leave the HAT's GND jumper on it and solder the button's wire to the same GND pin from the underside.
  3. Keep the button leads short, and never route them through the HAT's power wires. (The Feather's own SW38 button isn't used: it would be hidden inside the frame.)
  1. One button leg → D0 (left side, 1st pin from the top). No resistor: the firmware turns on the pull-up.
  2. Other leg → GND. Leave the HAT's GND jumper where it is and solder the button's wire to the same GND pin from the underside of the XIAO.
  3. Keep the button leads short too, and never route them through the HAT's power wires.

Optional: a toggle switch from D9 to GND still works as a config-mode switch, but you don't need it.

4Battery

✅ No soldering, no resistors. The battery plugs into the Feather's socket and charges whenever USB-C is connected (the CHG light is on while charging). The battery-level reading is already on the board: two 200 kΩ resistors feeding GPIO35, which the firmware reads by itself. Don't add the two-resistor bridge from the XIAO build.

The two connectors and the adapter

  1. Get an adapter: search "JST PH 2.0 male to 1.25 female" (sometimes listed as "PH2.0 to MX1.25" or "Molex PicoBlade"). Short is best. Or use an Adafruit LiPo, which plugs straight in.
  2. Check polarity before it touches the Feather (adapters and batteries aren't always wired the same way): plug battery into adapter, then put the multimeter on the adapter's PH 2.0 plug. Hold the plug the way it will go in. The pin that will sit next to the + printed by the Feather's socket must read +3.7 V (red probe on it, black on the other).
  3. Reads −3.7 V? The adapter is reversed. Don't plug it in: get another, or swap its two pins (lift the tiny tab on the housing with a pin and swap the wires).
  4. Plug in. Keep the leads short and away from the panel ribbon.
  5. Battery % appears in Home Assistant at the next check-in. Nothing to set: on the Feather the "battery reading fitted" setting is on by default.

No adapter to hand? Don't cut the battery's own leads (a slip shorts the cell). Instead join a 1.25 mm socket pigtail to a PH 2.0 plug pigtail, red to red and black to black, with solder and heat-shrink. That's wire-to-wire, nowhere near the board.

⚠️ Check the battery plug with a multimeter first: red probe on the wire you'll solder to BAT+ must read +3.7 V. Reversed = dead XIAO.

The easy way to solder the tiny pads

  1. Solder a JST-PH pigtail (socket with two short wires) to the pads, never the battery itself. The battery just plugs in afterwards.
  2. Tape the XIAO upside-down to the desk. Add flux, then put a small blob of solder on each pad (≈ 330 °C, chisel or bevel tip). Strip 2 mm of wire, twist, and tin it too.
  3. Tape the wire so its tinned tip lies flat on the pad by itself. Touch the iron on top for 2 s — the two blobs melt together. No third hand needed.
  4. Tug gently, check no bridge between pads, then cover with a dab of hot glue or Kapton so the wires can't flex at the joint.

Battery reading: two resistors, no capacitor (optional)

Lets Home Assistant show the battery %. Skip it and the frame still works; the battery just shows "unknown".

  1. Take two 100 kΩ resistors (220 kΩ also works). Twist one leg of each together and solder: that's the middle. Slide heat-shrink over it.
  2. Do this while soldering the battery pigtail: put the free leg of one resistor into the BAT+ joint with the red wire, and the other into the BAT− joint with the black wire. No extra pads to hit.
  3. Run a short wire from the middle to D3 (left side, 4th pin from the top when the XIAO is face-up). Easiest: cut a female jumper in half, solder it to the middle, push it onto D3.
  4. Hold the button 3 s → http://eink-frame.local → tick Battery divider fitted on D3 → Save.

It draws about 20 µA, roughly 6% of the battery per year. Don't use 1 MΩ resistors without the capacitor: the reading becomes unreliable.

Optional: steadier panel power with one capacitor

Photos with fine green, white or blue dots make the panel pull current in short, heavy bursts. If the board's small 3.3 V supply can't keep up, those colours collapse to yellow, red and black. A capacitor right at the HAT stores energy and covers the bursts. No soldering: its legs push into the socket on the HAT's underside.

Where it goes

Why it helps

  1. Unplug USB (and any battery) first.
  2. Find pin 1 on the HAT's underside socket: the end with a square pad or a printed "1". Pin 6 is the third pin along the other row from that end.
  3. Bend the legs so they reach: long leg (+) into pin 1, short leg / stripe side (−) into pin 6. Push them in firmly; they should hold by themselves. Leave pins 2 and 4 empty.
  4. Check twice that the stripe faces pin 6. A reversed electrolytic capacitor can bulge or pop.
  5. Plug USB back in and show a green-heavy photo. Optional extra: a 10 µF ceramic capacitor (either way round) in pins 17 (3.3 V) and 20 (GND) helps a little more.

Pins 1 and 6 are the same 3.3 V and GND the 10-pin cable's VCC and GND reach, so the capacitor sits right at the panel. If photos are still wrong with it, go for a separate 3.3 V regulator for the HAT instead.
